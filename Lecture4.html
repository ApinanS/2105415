Preliminary process synthesis

What are we going to do this semester?

Design I builds a plant in five decisions. They are taken in a fixed order, and today is the first one.

1

Decide whether the process runs as a batch or a continuous flow process.

2

Identify the input–output structure of the process.

3

Identify and design the general structure of each system.

Preliminary process synthesis

… and then, in far more detail

4

Identify and design the process equipment of each system.

5

Specify the operating condition of every equipment item.

Steps 2–5 are Lectures 5 onwards. None of them can start until step 1 is answered.

Why this decision leads

The mode fixes what every later number means

Step 1 is not a formality. Until the mode is fixed, the quantities in steps 2–5 do not even have units.

Amount

Continuous: a flowrate, kg/h.
Batch: a charge, kg per batch.

Equipment size

Continuous: sized on flow.
Batch: sized on one charge plus turnaround.

Control

Continuous: hold a steady state.
Batch: follow a recipe in time.

Lecture 4-1 · the starting point

Start from continuous, then justify leaving it

Normally a chemical process is designed to operate continuously.
  • So the real question is never “which mode?” — it is what would force us off continuous?
  • The rest of this section is that list of forcing reasons.

Lecture 4-1 · the list

Batch operation is considered when…

Every reason on the source list falls into one of three families. We take them in that order.

Scale

Production rate too small for a continuous plant to pay.

Process behaviour

Scale-up risk, long reaction times, slurries, fouling.

Product & market

Multiproduct, high quality, seasonal feed, short life.

Family 1 · production rate

Two screening numbers worth remembering

Below 5,000 t/y a plant is sometimes batch. Below 500 t/y it is usually batch.

Rules of thumb, not laws — they screen candidates, they do not decide.

  • Both numbers are annual tonnages of product, not of feed.

Family 1 · production rate

What 500 t/y actually looks like as a flow

$$\dot m = \frac{Q}{t_{\text{op}}}\qquad \dot V = \frac{\dot m}{\rho}$$

$t_{\text{op}}$ = operating hours per year (h/y); $\rho$ = liquid density (kg/m³).

$\dot m = \dfrac{5.0\times10^{5}}{8000} = 62.5\ \text{kg/h}$ 500 t/y in kg, over 8,000 operating hours
$\dot V = \dfrac{62.5}{900} = 0.069\ \text{m}^3\text{/h} \approx 1.2\ \text{L/min}$ divide by a typical liquid density, 900 kg/m³

Control valves and flowmeters become unreliable at a few litres per minute.

Family 3 · product and market

One plant, several products, one at a time

Tonnage was the first screen. The next reasons survive even when the tonnage is large.

  • A batch plant is vessels plus a recipe — the same equipment makes product A this week, product B next.
  • A continuous plant is welded into one product.
Most speciality and pharmaceutical plants are built this way.

Family 3 · product and market

A batch is a lot you can quarantine

  • Each batch has its own number, its own record and its own retained sample.
  • If one batch fails a test, you reject that batch — not a week of continuous production.
  • Regulators for medicines and food require exactly this traceability.

Family 2 · process behaviour

What worked in the laboratory may not scale

The remaining reasons come from the process itself — how the chemistry behaves in a large vessel.

  • Mixing time, heat removal per unit volume and wall effects all change with vessel size.
  • A stirred batch vessel can be scaled in modest steps, and a failed step costs one campaign.
  • A continuous train is committed on paper, years before it runs.

Family 2 · process behaviour

Solids stay suspended only above a critical velocity

  • Below that velocity the solids settle, the line blocks, and the plant stops.
  • A small continuous plant runs at exactly those low velocities.
  • A stirred batch vessel keeps the same solids suspended with an impeller, at any throughput.

Family 2 · rapidly fouling materials

In a batch plant, cleaning is already in the schedulecase 1 of 2

When there is significant equipment fouling, batch operations are favoured.
  • Cleaning is a standard operating procedure between batches, so it is accommodated easily in the schedule.
  • It costs turnaround time that the plan already allows for — not lost production.

Family 2 · rapidly fouling materials

In a continuous plant, cleaning costs a spare traincase 2 of 2

  • Significant fouling in continuous operation is serious and hard to handle.
  • Identical units in parallel — one on-line, one off-line for cleaning — solve it.
  • But capital investment is higher, additional labour is required, and safety problems are more likely.

Family 3 · market forces

A feedstock that exists for part of the year

The last two reasons come from outside the fence: from the feed market and the product market.

  • Sugar cane, palm fruit, cassava and fruit pulp arrive in a harvest window.
  • A continuous plant sized for that window stands idle the rest of the year, still paying its capital.
  • A batch plant campaigns the crop, then makes something else.

Family 3 · market forces

A product that dies before the plant is paid for

  • Electronic chemicals, agrochemicals and fine chemicals can be replaced within a few years.
  • A dedicated continuous plant has no second use when that happens.
  • A batch plant is redeployed to the next molecule with a new recipe.

Worked example — answer before you click

Batch or continuous? Build the argument

A company will make a new speciality antioxidant: 800 t/y, three grades from the same chemistry, reaction time 9 h, product expected to be replaced in about five years. Which mode, and which criterion decides it?
Batch. Four criteria agree: 800 t/y sits in the “sometimes batch” band, three grades means multiproduct, 9 h needs a large continuous reactor, and a five-year life will not repay a dedicated train.

Lecture 4-1 · summary

What section 4-1 settled

  • Continuous is the default, because capital per tonne falls as $Q^{-0.4}$.
  • Leave the default only for a named reason: scale, process behaviour, or market.
  • Screen on tonnage first — 500 and 5,000 t/y — then on reaction time and fouling.
  • Fouling and long reaction times are paid for in duplicated or oversized plant.

Introduction · problem statement

A refinery stream nobody wants to sell

  • Benzene, toluene and xylenes (BTX) are upstream products of the petrochemical industry.
  • They are made from naphtha by catalytic reforming, then separated and purified into high-grade products.
  • The separation also leaves a contaminated by-product stream — several compounds mixed together.

Introduction · problem statement

Where that stream comes from

Our plant starts where the refinery stops caring — at the red stream.

Introduction · choosing a route

Three routes exist; this design takes one

The by-product can be upgraded by additional processes. The brief names three and fixes one.

Hydrodealkylation

Strip alkyl groups with hydrogen. Our route.

Isomerization

Rearrange one xylene isomer into another.

Dismutation

Two toluenes give one benzene and one xylene.

Reaction information

Seconds, not hoursstep 2 of 2

From the previous slide$\text{GHSV}=\dot V_0/V_{\text{cat}}$

Product specification · consequence

The separation, not the reaction, is the hard part

From the spec table$w_{\mathrm{B}} \ge 99.90\ \text{wt \%},\quad w_{\mathrm{T}} \le 500\ \text{wt ppm}$

Design basis

The tonnage the plant is designed around

Design basis

What 350,000 t/y demands at the gate

From 4-1, and mass conservation$\dot m = \dfrac{Q}{t_{\text{op}}},\qquad \dot m_{\text{in}} = \dfrac{\dot m_{\mathrm{B}}}{w_{\mathrm{B,out}}}$

Lecture 4-2 · summary

What section 4-2 settled

  • A design brief is feeds, reaction data, product spec and a tonnage — nothing else is given.
  • Conversions are read from the inlet–outlet table; a product's loss uses what was made as basis.
  • Four reactions reproduce all nine rows, so the proposed pathways survive testing.
  • At 350,000 t/y with a 46 s contact time, every 4-1 criterion says continuous.

Decide the operating mode before any other design number.

Screen on tonnage first, then on reaction time and fouling.

4

State the basis before you quote any conversion.

Test a proposed pathway on a row you did not fit.
