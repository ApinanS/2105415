Starting point

A feed stream is not a pure component

The black box needs feed streams. Real feed streams arrive with company.

Reactant

The component converted into the desired product by the reaction.

Impurity

Everything else that enters with that feed stream.

Every feed stream splits into exactly these two. Nothing in a feed is unclassified.

Definitions — the split that matters

An impurity is reactive, or it is inert

Reactive impurity

It reacts, but it does not make the desired product.

Inert impurity

It crosses the reactor unchanged.

One molecule is a reactant here and an impurity next door. Read the chemistry, not the label.

Definitions — the other side of the reactor

Product, and everything else generated

Product

The component the plant is built to sell.

By-product

Anything else a reaction generates.

A by-product is not a mistake. It is revenue, fuel or a disposal bill — and you must decide which.

The only real decision

Should we purify a feed stream before it enters the process?

Purify

One extra unit up front, then a clean loop.

Process it

No unit up front, but every unit downstream grows.

What section 5-1 settled

Two words engineers use loosely — and must not

Vapor is condensable, gas is not

Section 5-1 sorted the feed by chemistry. The output structure sorts by phase — so the words have to be exact.

The working rule in this course

Which components do we call gases?

Judging every component against its own $T_C$ is slow. One cut-off does it in a glance.

A component boiling below propylene (−48 °C) is treated as a gaseous compound.
Judge it at the conditions of the output stream you are designing, not at the reactor.

The consequence you will use all lecture

Gases are expensive to separate

Hard to condense

No condensation means no cheap split.

So they travel together

All gases share one exit stream.

So sell a liquid

Take the primary product out as liquid.

General considerations for distributing chemicals

Safety comes before economics1 of 3

The five steps produce a diagram. Three general rules decide whether it is a diagram you may build.

  • Choose raw materials and reactions that avoid hazardous or toxic chemicals.
  • Reduce what must be stored when you cannot avoid it.
Hazard data can rule out a raw material before any calculation starts.

General considerations for distributing chemicals

Remove the inert, or keep it?2 of 3

  • For a nearly pure product, remove inert species before the reaction.
  • But keep the inert when a large exothermic heat must be removed.
An inert diluent is a free heat sink; deleting it can make cooling impossible.

General considerations for distributing chemicals

What deserves a purge3 of 3

  • Purge species that arrive as feed impurities or form irreversibly — traces, or hard to separate.
  • Light species purge as vapor; heavy species purge as liquid.
  • Never purge valuable, toxic or hazardous species. Recover the value; destroy the hazard.

Case 6 — midterm examination 2024 · propylene oxide from propylene and oxygen

The problem

  • Design a process making propylene oxide, the intermediate that becomes polyurethane, from propylene and oxygen over a silver catalyst.
  • The feed gas is propylene and oxygen with nitrogen as an inert diluent, which keeps the mixture out of the explosive range.
  • The nitrogen to oxygen molar ratio must stay above 4 : 1.
  • Only an experimental reactor result is given — no reaction pathways at all.

Case 6 · what the experimental reactor was

Reaction and experimental reactor information

  • Fixed-bed reactor, catalyst bed 1.23 cm diameter and 1 m long.
  • 200 °C, 5 bar, gas-phase reaction.
  • Catalyst: silver on alumina.
  • Selectivity to propylene oxide rises with temperature, but too high and you make more CO₂.
  • Oxygen must be controlled to stop combustion and over-oxidation of the product.
  • Water in the effluent cannot be measured, so it is found by mass balance.
  • Propylene to oxygen is held at 5 : 1 by mole, in the laboratory and in the plant.
  • In the laboratory, nitrogen to oxygen is 4 : 1.

Case 6 · raw material specifications, and what they force

Design basis and assumptions

  • Propylene: 99 wt %, ambient temperature, 10 bar.
  • Air: 20 mol % O₂, 79 mol % N₂, 1 mol % CO₂, at 2 bar — with extra nitrogen added.
  • Nitrogen: 99 wt %, 150 bar.
  • Propylene boils at 20 bar when it is at 50 °C, so it is stored and handled as a liquid.
  • Basis: 100 g/h of oxygen fed to the process; the target is 160,000 t/y of product.
  • Steady state; overall oxygen conversion 0.99, and the rest vents.
  • Propylene is recycled to extinction, so none of it leaves.
  • Air alone gives N₂/O₂ = 3.95, so pure nitrogen must top it up to 4 : 1.

Case 6 · question 1 · five marks that almost nobody scored

“Why is oxygen the limiting reactant?”

Explain it in terms of what the oxygen would do, not in terms of the feed ratio.
  • Oxygen in excess drives combustion of the propylene to carbon dioxide and water.
  • It also over-oxidises the propylene oxide itself, destroying the product you came for.
  • Making oxygen the limiting reactant is therefore a selectivity decision, not an arithmetic one.
  • Quoting the 5 : 1 propylene-to-oxygen ratio only restates the specification. It is not the reason.

Case 6 · what the cohort actually wrote

Five ways this paper was lost

  • Recycle and purge for the gases. Air is a cheap reactant feed: vent it, do not build a gas loop.
  • Recycling the nitrogen. It is an inert diluent, not something to recover.
  • Propylene called a gas reactant. At −48 °C and 10 bar storage it is a liquid.
  • No added nitrogen. Air alone cannot hold N₂/O₂ at 4 : 1, and the balance will not close without it.
  • CO₂ left out of the feed. Industrial air carries 1 mol % of it.
  • Reactor and process balances mixed. Water comes from the reactor totals; the plant rates come from scaling.

Section summary

What the six cases settled

  • Case 1 — the primary product decides the gas–liquid line, and a large saleable by-product earns its own stream.
  • Case 2 — with more than one reactor, a recycle code must name the reactor it returns to.
  • Case 3 — the destination codes come first: a vented reactant cannot be fed near stoichiometry.
  • Case 4 — feed minus product gives every role, and you scale on what you are contracted to deliver.
  • Case 5 — a recycled excess reactant is no excess at all at the process boundary.
  • Case 6 — an inert fed for safety still crosses the plant, and it still has to be paid for and sized.
