6-1

Lecture 6-1

What are the basic systems inside a chemical process?

Scope

The plant is bigger than the process

Lecture 6 balances only the process block. Everything around it is sized after its streams are known.

Structure

Every chemical process has the same four blocks

We fixed what we balance. Now the inside of that block — the same in almost every plant.

Structure

What each block is for

Reactor feed preparation

Sets temperature, pressure and phase. Removes catalyst poisons.

Reactor

The only block where bonds change.

Phase separation

Splits the effluent into gas and liquid.

Purification

Splits the liquid into product, recycle and fuel.

Structure

Two recycles, because there are two phases

Phase separation splits the effluent in two. Each half carries unconverted reactant, so each half needs its own recycle.

Gas recycle

Returns unconverted gas reactant. Gases separate badly, so a purge stops impurities building up.

Liquid recycle

Returns unconverted liquid reactant. Distillation is sharp, so no purge is needed.

Move 2 of 2

Destination code: how it gets out

Primary product

Leaves at sales purity. The basis of the balance.

Recycle

Returned to the reactor, so process conversion is 1.

Recycle and purge

Recycled, with a slip stream so inerts cannot build up.

Fuel / VBP

Value-by-product: burnt, or sold below product value.

Section 6-1

What section 6-1 settled

  • The process is four blocks: feed preparation, reactor, phase separation, purification — plus one recycle per phase.
  • Every component is classified by boiling point and by its role in the reactions.
  • The destination code, not the chemistry, is what fixes how many streams leave.
  • Changing one code changes the flowsheet — which is why alternatives exist.

What must we recall before balancing a process with a recycle stream?

Reference figure

Idea 1 of 4

Limiting reactant

The reactant that runs out first if the reaction goes to completion.

It is present in less than its stoichiometric proportion relative to every other reactant.

In practice the expensive reactant is made limiting.

Idea 2 of 4

The plant converts more than the reactor does

$$F_r=F+R,\qquad F=x\,F_r\ \Rightarrow\ R=F_r(1-x)$$Mixing point, then steady state.
$$\frac{R}{F}=\frac{1-x}{x}$$

$x$ single-pass conversion; $F$ fresh feed, $F_r$ reactor feed, $R$ recycle, t/y.

What it proves: below about $x=0.3$ the recycle grows faster than any saving in reactor volume.

Idea 2 of 4 · consequence

For the overall balance, take conversion as 1

$$\text{Single-pass conversion}=\frac{\text{reactant consumed in reaction}}{\text{reactant fed to the reactor}}$$
$$\text{Overall conversion}=\frac{\text{reactant consumed in process}}{\text{reactant fed to the process}}$$

To do the overall material balance (around the process), the 100 % conversion of the limiting reactant is used.

Overall conversion = process conversion.

Idea 3 of 4

When reactions compete, conversion is not enough

QuantityGeneral formTextbook form
Conversion $X$$\dfrac{F_{A0}-F_A}{F_{A0}}$same
Yield $Y$$\dfrac{F_R}{F_{A0}}$$\dfrac{F_R}{F_{A0}-F_A}$
Selectivity $S$$\dfrac{F_R}{F_{A0}-F_A}$$\dfrac{F_R}{F_S}$

$F_{A0}$ fed, $F_A$ left, $F_R$ desired product, $F_S$ unwanted product, mol/s.

Idea 3 of 4 · with stoichiometry

Divide by the stoichiometric coefficient

Competing scheme$$\mathrm{A}+2\mathrm{B}\rightarrow 4\mathrm{C}+\mathrm{D}\quad(1)\qquad 2\mathrm{A}+\mathrm{B}\rightarrow \mathrm{U}+3\mathrm{G}\quad(2)$$
$$S=\frac{F_C/\nu_{1C}}{(F_{A0}-F_A)/(-\nu_{1A})}\qquad Y=\frac{F_C/\nu_{1C}}{F_{A0}/(-\nu_{1A})}$$

$F$ is a molar flowrate (mol/s), not a concentration. $\nu_{1C}$ is the coefficient of C in reaction 1 — the subscript names the reaction.

Idea 4 of 4

Excess: chosen at the reactor, solved at the process

$$\%\,\text{excess}=\frac{\text{supplied}-\text{stoi}}{\text{stoi}}\times 100$$
Quoted atStreamSet byTypical
Reactor inletS4, mixed gas feedkinetics and safety30 %
Process inletS2, fresh gas feedwhat leaves in the purge0 – 10 %

Input–output structure: the overall balance assumes no excess reactant at the process inlet — % excess = 0.

General process design: an excess gas reactant cannot stay at 0 % — some of it leaves in the purge.

Back-calculate it from the specified % purge — about 5–10 % of the gas leaving phase separation.

Section 6-2

What section 6-2 settled

  • Quote conversion with its envelope: per reactor pass, or per pass through the plant.
  • With a recycle, $R/F=(1-x)/x$, and a recycled reactant has overall conversion 1.
  • When reactions compete, report selectivity too — and say which convention.
  • Excess at the reactor is chosen; excess at the process inlet is solved from the purge.

Lecture 6-3

How do we do a material balance with a recycle stream, using the chemical pathway?

Chemistry

Hydrodealkylation is four reactions, not one

Main (1)$\mathrm{C_7H_8}+\mathrm{H_2}\rightarrow \mathrm{C_6H_6}+\mathrm{CH_4}$toluene → benzene
Reaction (2)$\mathrm{C_8H_{10}}+\mathrm{H_2}\rightarrow \mathrm{C_6H_6}+\mathrm{C_2H_6}$ethylbenzene → benzene
Reaction (3)$\mathrm{C_8H_{10}}+\mathrm{H_2}\rightarrow \mathrm{C_7H_8}+\mathrm{CH_4}$m-xylene → toluene
Side (4)$2\,\mathrm{C_6H_6}\rightarrow \mathrm{C_{12}H_{10}}+\mathrm{H_2}$benzene → biphenyl

The one that shapes everything: reaction 3 makes toluene, so toluene is both fed and generated.

What section 6-3 settled

When do we write down a process alternative?

The observation

The purge is almost pure methane

Section 6-3 finished with a converged flowsheet. Before accepting it, look at what the recycle actually carries.

$$\frac{f^{S12}_{\mathrm{Hy}}}{f^{S12}}=\frac{1{,}029}{80{,}270}=1.3\,\%$$

At 10 % excess. The loop gas is 94 wt % methane — the thing we meant to purge.

The rule

An uncertain decision becomes an alternative

We recycle 490,000 t/y of gas to recover 6,300 t/y of hydrogen. Is that right?

Not certain a decision is correct? Record the opposite as a process alternative and cost both.

Lecture 6-6

How many reactor systems are required?

Consideration of the reactor system

Before sizing anything, ask two things

How many reactor systems?

One block on the flowsheet may have to become two.

Separate between them?

If there are two, must something be removed in between?

The rule

Count operating conditions, not reactions

  • A single reaction system needs one reactor system.
  • Multiple reactions need more only when their conditions differ.
  • The three that force a split: temperature, pressure, catalyst.

Hydrodealkylation: four reactions, one reactor — all at 640 °C and 35 bar.

A second case

Acetic anhydride from acetone and acetic acid

The rule needs a case where it bites. This one has three reactions and two temperatures.

Reaction (1)$\mathrm{CH_3COCH_3}\rightarrow \mathrm{CH_2CO}+\mathrm{CH_4}$acetone → ketene + methane700 °C, 1 atm
Side (2)$\mathrm{CH_2CO}\rightarrow \mathrm{CO}+\tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathrm{C_2H_4}$ketene → carbon monoxide + ethylene700 °C, 1 atm
Reaction (3)$\mathrm{CH_2CO}+\mathrm{CH_3COOH}\rightarrow \mathrm{(CH_3CO)_2O}$ketene + acetic acid → acetic anhydride80 °C, 1 atm

The pivot: ketene $\mathrm{CH_2CO}$ is made at 700 °C and consumed at 80 °C.

Applying the rule

620 degrees apart

Reaction (1) at 700 °C$$\mathrm{CH_3COCH_3}\rightarrow\mathrm{CH_2CO}+\mathrm{CH_4}$$
Reaction (3) at 80 °C$$\mathrm{CH_2CO}+\mathrm{CH_3COOH}\rightarrow\mathrm{(CH_3CO)_2O}$$

At 700 °C the anhydride decomposes; at 80 °C the acetone does not crack. No single temperature serves both.

Answer: two reactor systems. Call them R1 at 700 °C and R2 at 80 °C.

Case 1 · three routes compete for the same glycerol

Reaction information at 150 °C and 10 bar

$$\underset{\text{Glycerol}}{\text{C}_3\text{H}_8\text{O}_3}+\tfrac{1}{2}\text{O}_2\rightarrow\underset{\text{Lactic acid}}{\text{C}_3\text{H}_6\text{O}_3}+\text{H}_2\text{O}\qquad \Delta H=-280\qquad(1)$$
$$\underset{\text{Glycerol}}{\text{C}_3\text{H}_8\text{O}_3}+\text{O}_2\rightarrow\underset{\text{Glyceric acid}}{\text{C}_3\text{H}_6\text{O}_4}+\text{H}_2\text{O}\qquad \Delta H=-456\qquad(2)$$

$\Delta H$ in kJ per mole of glycerol. Single-pass conversion 95 %; selectivities 63 / 22 / 15 mol %.

Case 1 · the rest of the reactor information, and what it forces you to assume

Design basis and assumptions

  1. Single-pass glycerol conversion 95 %. On converted glycerol the selectivities are lactic acid (LA) 63 %, glyceric acid (GA) 22 %, complete oxidation 15 mol %.
  2. Reactor feed is held at 20 wt % glycerol, reached by diluting the 80/20 purified feed with deionized water.
  3. Glycerol decomposes at its normal boiling point, so vacuum distillation at 0.30 bara, where it boils at 235 °C, recovers it for recycle.
  • Basis: 60,000 t/y of the purified feed, so 48,000 t/y of glycerol.
  • Steady state, and item 7 means glycerol is recycled to extinction — overall conversion is 1.
  • Item 4 fixes the oxygen at the reactor, not at the process boundary.
  • Oxygen leaves through a vent, so the process excess is a result, not a choice.

Case 1 · question 2 · what the marking found

Three mistakes that cost most of the marks

The dilution water

More than 80 % of answers never fed water to bring the reactor feed to 20 wt % glycerol — and 190,105 t/y is the largest single feed in the plant.

Which reaction sets the oxygen

The stoichiometric oxygen is taken on reaction (1) only, not on all three. The excess is quoted against that number.

The process excess

Assuming 0 % or 10 % excess at the boundary is wrong here: oxygen is vented, so the boundary excess is whatever the reactor and recycle leave — 216 %.

Key Takeaways

1

Give every component a destination code before you calculate anything.

Do the overall balance first — process conversion is 1 for a recycled reactant.

3

Follow the chemical pathway, not the reaction list: count regenerated reactants twice.

Tear one stream, march the loop, drive the difference to zero.

Write down the opposite of every decision you are not sure of.
